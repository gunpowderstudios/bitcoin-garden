Bitcoin in plain English

Bitcoin is digital money you can send over the internet without a bank having to approve the payment. Bitcoin is the name of the network, and bitcoin (BTC) is the money that moves on it. Instead of one company keeping the accounts, computers around the world can keep and check their own copies of a shared public record.

Why do people call it “digital gold”? Gold is scarce in nature. Bitcoin is scarce by design: its rules cap the total at 21 million BTC. That’s the most that can ever be created, not how many are in circulation today. The nickname is about scarcity, not a promise that Bitcoin will hold its value.

The clever bit: you can’t spend the same bitcoin twice

You can copy a photo and send it to two people. That’s great for photos, but disastrous for money. Banks solve this by keeping the official record of who paid whom. Bitcoin’s trick is getting a network of strangers to agree on a payment history without one official bookkeeper.

  1. You sign

    Your wallet uses a private key to sign a payment and prove you’re allowed to spend those funds, without revealing the key itself.

  2. Miners do the work

    Miners compete to add a batch of payments, called a block. Finding a valid answer takes computing power and electricity. Checking it is quick. That’s called proof of work.

  3. The network checks

    Computers called nodes independently check the rules. Each block links to the previous one, so rewriting old payments means redoing the work and catching up with the rest of the network.

What if I try to pay a baker and a bookshop with the same bitcoin?

Only one of those payments can be recorded in the same valid history. Once the baker’s payment is in that history, the nodes reject a later attempt to spend the same funds at the bookshop. That’s what people mean by double-spending.

How it started

  1. 31 Oct 2008
    An idea lands in an inbox

    Someone using the name Satoshi Nakamoto shares a nine-page paper on a cryptography mailing list. It proposes a way to send electronic cash directly between people. Satoshi’s real identity remains unconfirmed. See the original email.

  2. 3 Jan 2009
    The first block has a British newspaper headline

    Bitcoin’s first entry, the Genesis Block, contains a headline from The Times about a possible second bank bailout. It’s a date stamp from the middle of the financial crisis. Whether Satoshi intended it as a political message is open to interpretation. Explore the Genesis Block.

  3. 12 Jan 2009
    One person pays another

    Satoshi sends 10 BTC to software developer Hal Finney in the first recorded person-to-person Bitcoin transfer. It’s a test that proves the software can do what the paper proposed.

    What were those 10 BTC worth? In January 2009, Bitcoin had no recorded exchange rate, so there’s no known cash value for that first transfer. By October 2009, an early published rate put 10 BTC at less than one US cent. Read about the first transfer.

Pizza, coffee and tiny fractions

🍕 Two pizzas for 10,000 BTC

On 22 May 2010, programmer Laszlo Hanyecz reported that he’d traded 10,000 BTC for two pizzas. A forum member took the bitcoin and arranged the delivery. Bitcoin fans mark 22 May as Bitcoin Pizza Day. Read the original pizza thread.

☕ An ATM in a coffee shop

In 2013, a Vancouver coffee shop became home to a Bitcoin ATM. It let customers exchange cash for bitcoin or sell bitcoin for cash. It wasn’t a bank branch. It was Waves Coffee. See the machine.

100 million sats

One bitcoin is made up of 100 million satoshis, or “sats”, named after Bitcoin’s creator. Think pounds and pennies, but with much smaller fractions.

21 million BTC

That’s the supply limit under Bitcoin’s rules. The number of new bitcoins awarded for adding a block halves roughly every four years, an event known as the “halving”. Read about the supply rules.

What’s the catch?

The public record is searchable, so Bitcoin isn’t automatically anonymous. If you manage your own keys and lose access to them without a backup, there’s no central helpdesk to restore your wallet. Mining takes electricity, and the price in pounds can swing sharply. The clever technology and the price are two different things.
